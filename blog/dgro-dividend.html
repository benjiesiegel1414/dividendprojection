Are DGRO dividends qualified? Yes, and here's why

Every year iShares publishes the share of each fund's dividend income that counts as qualified. For the 2025 tax year, $DGRO came in at 100.00%. Same for its cousins $HDV and $DVY.

That isn't luck. It's baked into the index. $DGRO tracks the Morningstar US Dividend Growth Index, and the index rules exclude real estate investment trusts specifically because the dividends need to be qualified income. REIT payouts are mostly taxed as ordinary income, so leaving them out keeps the fund's income clean for taxable accounts.

What qualified means in plain English:

  • In a taxable account: qualified dividends are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on your income, the same rates as long-term capital gains. Non-qualified dividends get taxed like your paycheck.
  • The holding period still applies to you: you generally need to have owned your $DGRO shares for more than 60 days during the 121-day window around the ex-dividend date. Buy the day before the ex-date and sell a week later, and that payment won't count as qualified for you.
  • In an IRA or 401(k): none of this matters until you withdraw. Roth money isn't taxed at all if you follow the rules.

This is the part most people miss: a fund's percentage can change from year to year. 100% in 2025 is a great sign, but your 1099-DIV is always the final word for your account. Box 1b shows the qualified portion.

How DGRO picks its stocks

Understanding the rules tells you what kind of income you're actually getting. The Morningstar US Dividend Growth Index requires a company to have:

  • At least five years of uninterrupted annual dividend growth. That's a lower bar than $VIG's ten, so $DGRO can catch companies earlier in their dividend growth story.
  • A payout ratio under 75% and a positive earnings forecast, so the dividend isn't eating the whole profit.
  • A yield that is not in the top 10% of the universe. This is the yield trap filter. The very highest yielders often have a dividend cut coming.

Then the index weights stocks by the dividend dollars they pay, not just by size. It's reconstituted once a year and rebalanced every quarter. Right now financials, technology, health care, consumer staples and industrials are the biggest sectors.

I like this setup. It's not chasing yield, it's chasing companies that keep raising their payouts while still having room to keep doing it. If you want to see how that plays out over 10 or 20 years with reinvesting, run it in the $DGRO dividend calculator.

My take: who DGRO is for

I've been a dividend investor for over a decade, and funds like $DGRO are the kind of thing I wish I'd understood earlier. It's boring in the best way.

  • If you hold dividends in a taxable account: $DGRO is one of the cleaner choices out there. 100% qualified income in 2025 and no REITs by design.
  • If you're building toward income 10+ years out: the low starting yield matters less than the growth. Reinvest and let the payout compound.
  • If you need high income today: a 2% yield won't get you there on its own. People in that spot usually pair a core like $DGRO with something that pays more, often monthly. My sister site MonthlyETFs.com tracks those.

What I'd avoid is dumping a steady grower like $DGRO for a 15% yielder just because the number looks bigger. High yield with a share price that keeps sliding can leave you with less income later, not more.
