The SPDR S&P Dividend ETF launched on November 8, 2005, and it's one of the original "dividend aristocrat" funds. The idea is simple: only own companies that have raised their dividend every single year for at least 20 years, then lean toward the ones that pay the most.

If you already own $SCHD or $VYM, $SDY is the fund people usually compare them to. It pays a little less today, but it's built around a very different question: not "who pays the most right now?" but "who has proven they'll keep raising the check?"

When is the next SDY dividend?

$SDY is predictable. Every 2026 payment has gone ex-dividend on the Monday after the third Friday of the quarter's last month: March 23, June 22 and September 21. Last December it went ex on Monday, December 22, 2025.

The third Friday of December 2026 is December 18, so I'd expect the next ex-date on Monday, December 21, 2026, with the cash a few days later. State Street hasn't announced the amount yet.

One pattern worth knowing: December pays the most. It was $1.03 in 2024 and $1.017 in 2025, versus $0.71 to $0.82 for the March checks. If you're budgeting quarter by quarter, the March payment is the light one.

From 2022 to 2025 the payout grew about 14%, or roughly 4.4% a year. That's not explosive. It's the kind of steady raise you'd expect from a basket of companies that have each raised their own dividend for two decades or more.

Compare that to an option income fund like $JEPI, whose payout dropped about a third after 2022. Different engine entirely. $SDY's check grows because the companies inside raise theirs. Slow, but it compounds.

One honest note: $SDY's payout isn't guaranteed to rise every year just because its holdings must have a 20-year streak. When a company cuts its dividend, it gets dropped at the next review, and yield weighting means the mix shifts. Look at the multi-year trend, not any single quarter.

How SDY picks its stocks

$SDY tracks the S&P High Yield Dividend Aristocrats Index. Here's how it works, straight from State Street's description:

  • The universe is the S&P Composite 1500, so large, mid and small caps are all eligible. That's wider than $NOBL, which only looks at the S&P 500.
  • The screen is at least 20 consecutive years of dividend increases. No streak, no entry.
  • Stocks are weighted by yield, not market cap. The higher a company's yield, the bigger its slice, with weights adjusted each quarter.
  • The lineup is reviewed every January. Companies that cut or freeze their dividend are removed.

This is the part most people miss: the yield weighting is what makes $SDY different from a plain dividend growth fund like $VIG. $VIG weights by size, so the giants dominate. $SDY tilts toward the higher-yielding names among the long-time raisers, which gives you more income today but also a heavier tilt toward slower-growing sectors like utilities and consumer staples.

The result is a fund that sits between pure income and pure growth. State Street puts it well: because of the 20-year screen, these stocks have both capital growth and dividend income characteristics, as opposed to stocks that are pure yield.

A few things jump out. $SCHD pays more and has a much lower fee, which is why it's the default dividend ETF for so many people; my SCHD review covers how it picks stocks. $VYM is the cheapest and the most diversified, at a similar yield to $SDY. $NOBL is the closest cousin, with the same 0.35% fee, but it's limited to the S&P 500 and weights its holdings equally instead of by yield. $DVY pays the most but doesn't require a dividend growth streak.

The fee is $SDY's weak spot. At 0.35%, you pay $35 a year per $10,000, versus $6 for $SCHD or $4 for $VYM. Over decades, that adds up. What you get for it is the strictest dividend-raiser screen in the group and a broader universe that includes mid and small caps.

My take: who SDY is for

I've been building dividend income for more than ten years, and the funds I trust most are the ones with a boring, repeatable rule. $SDY has one of the best: prove you've raised your dividend for 20 years, or you're out.

  • If you want income that grows: $SDY is a solid pick. The payout has risen about 4% to 5% a year recently, and the yield-weighted, 154-stock portfolio spreads the risk out.
  • If you care most about cost: $SCHD and $VYM do a similar job for a fraction of the fee. That's a real advantage over 20 or 30 years.
  • If you're heavy in tech already: $SDY's tilt toward industrials, utilities and staples is a genuine diversifier next to an S&P 500 or Nasdaq fund.
  • If you need high income now: a 2.4% yield won't get you there. That's a different kind of fund.

SDY dividend FAQ

How often does SDY pay dividends?

$SDY pays quarterly, four times a year. It goes ex-dividend in the second half of March, June, September and December, usually on the Monday after the third Friday of the month, and pays a few business days later.

When is the next SDY dividend?

The most recent $SDY dividend was $0.917 per share, ex-dividend Monday, September 21, 2026. Based on its pattern, the next one should go ex-dividend around Monday, December 21, 2026. December is usually the biggest payment of the year.

What is SDY's dividend yield?

$SDY's dividend yield is currently about 2.3%, based on the TopDividendETFsPRO database, which updates daily. It paid $3.78 per share over the last 12 months, and State Street listed its 30-day SEC yield at 2.30% as of November 10, 2026.

Is SDY a dividend aristocrat ETF?

Yes, in a stricter way than most. $SDY tracks the S&P High Yield Dividend Aristocrats Index, which only includes S&P Composite 1500 companies that have raised their dividend for at least 20 consecutive years, weighted by yield. The better-known $NOBL uses a 25-year rule but only looks at S&P 500 companies and weights them equally.

How much does $10,000 in SDY pay?

At the current yield, $10,000 in $SDY pays about $230 a year, or roughly $57.50 each quarter. The December check is usually the largest and the March check the smallest.
