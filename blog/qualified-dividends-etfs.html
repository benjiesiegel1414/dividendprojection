Are ETF dividends qualified? VOO, SCHD, JEPI, SGOV and more (2026)

Plain stock index ETFs like $VOO, $SCHD and $DGRO paid almost entirely qualified dividends in 2025. Option income funds like $JEPI and $JEPQ mostly did not, and Treasury funds like $SGOV paid zero qualified dividends. Here are the real 2025 numbers from each issuer, and what they mean for the money you actually keep.

Short answer: it depends on where the income comes from. In 2025, $SCHD paid 98.48% qualified, $VOO about 97% and $DGRO 100%. But $JEPI was only 15.86% qualified, $JEPQ 5.10%, and $SGOV 0%. $QQQI and $SPYI were mostly return of capital, a different thing entirely.

I get this question a lot, usually phrased as "is $X a qualified dividend?" The honest answer is that "qualified" isn't a property of an ETF. It's a property of each dollar the ETF pays you, and it depends on what the fund owns and how it earns its income.

The good news is that issuers publish the exact split every year. So instead of guessing, I pulled the 2025 tax documents for eight of the most searched income ETFs and put the numbers side by side.

What makes a dividend "qualified"?

A qualified dividend is a dividend from a U.S. company (or a qualifying foreign one) that you held long enough. The IRS says qualified dividends get the same 0%, 15% or 20% maximum rates that apply to long-term capital gains. Everything else is an ordinary dividend, taxed at your regular income tax rate, the same as your paycheck.

For 2026, here's where those rates kick in, based on taxable income:

This is the part most people miss: a married couple with up to $98,900 of taxable income can pay 0% federal tax on qualified dividends. That's a big reason I care so much about which kind of dividend a fund pays when I hold it outside a retirement account.

On your 1099-DIV, Box 1a is total ordinary dividends and Box 1b is the qualified part. The gap between the two is what gets taxed at your full rate.

Look at the pattern. The lower the yield, the more qualified the dividend tends to be. The funds paying 8% to 14% get there with option premium and other income, and that income doesn't get the qualified label.

VOO, SCHD and DGRO: nearly all qualified

These funds just own regular U.S. companies and pass along the dividends those companies pay. That's the textbook qualified dividend.

  • $VOO: Vanguard's 2025 tax data shows $7.0678 a share in ordinary dividends, of which $6.8671 was qualified. That's about 97.2%. The other $0.20 was Section 199A dividends from the REITs inside the S&P 500. And $VOO paid no capital gains distributions in 2025. My full $VOO dividend breakdown has the schedule.
  • $SCHD: Schwab reported a 98.48% qualified average for 2025. My $SCHD review covers what it holds.
  • $DGRO: iShares reported 100.00% qualified for 2025. The index skips REITs on purpose, which is why it's a clean 100%. I wrote a whole post on why $DGRO's dividends are qualified.

Why isn't $VOO a perfect 100%? REITs. Real estate trusts don't pay corporate tax, so their dividends usually aren't qualified. They do often get the separate Section 199A treatment, which lets many people deduct part of that income. Any fund that owns REITs will have a slice like this.

JEPI and JEPQ: mostly ordinary income

J.P. Morgan's 2025 distribution notice shows 15.86% of $JEPI's income was qualified, and only 5.10% of $JEPQ's.

Both funds own stocks, so some of the income is real stock dividends and qualifies. But most of the payout comes from selling call options, which they do through equity-linked notes. That option income is generally treated as ordinary income. And since $JEPQ owns Nasdaq companies that pay tiny dividends, almost all of its check is the option part. Hence 5%.

So if you're in the 22% or 24% bracket, most of a $JEPQ check gets taxed at 22% or 24%, not 15%. That matters a lot at an 11.7% yield. More on the $JEPQ dividend schedule here.

SGOV: 0% qualified, but there's a state tax perk

$SGOV owns Treasury bills, and interest is never a qualified dividend. iShares reported 0.00% qualified for 2025. So yes, $SGOV dividends are taxable, at your ordinary federal rate.

The upside: iShares reported that 95.14% of $SGOV's 2025 income came from U.S. government obligations, and the fund met the test of holding at least 50% federal obligations at each quarter end. Many states let you exclude that portion from state income tax. If you live in a high tax state, that can make $SGOV beat a bank account or money market fund paying the same rate. Rules vary by state, so check yours.

QQQI and SPYI: mostly return of capital

The NEOS funds are a different animal. Their 2025 Form 8937 filings classify about 99% of $QQQI's distributions and about 95% of $SPYI's as return of capital.

Return of capital isn't a qualified or an ordinary dividend. It's not taxed when you receive it. Instead it lowers your cost basis, so you pay later, as a capital gain, when you sell. That's tax deferral, not tax free.

NEOS also uses index options, which are Section 1256 contracts taxed 60% long-term and 40% short-term, no matter how long they were held. That's a big part of why these funds get talked about as tax efficient. My $QQQI review and $SPYI review go deeper on how the income is made.

One caution: return of capital can also simply mean the fund is paying out more than it earns. Look at total return, not just the yield. ETFTotalReturns.com lines that up for most income ETFs.

On $JEPI, the tax drag is about 21% of the income. On $SCHD it's about 15%. Not the end of the world, but over 20 years of compounding, that gap adds up. You can model it in the dividend calculator by trimming the yield to your after-tax number.

And none of this matters in an IRA, 401(k) or Roth. Inside those, qualified vs ordinary is irrelevant until you withdraw (and never, in a Roth).

The 60-day rule that can cost you

Even a 100% qualified fund only pays you a qualified dividend if you meet the holding period. Per IRS Publication 550, you must hold the shares more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date.

So if you buy $SCHD a week before the ex-date and sell a week after, that dividend is ordinary income for you. For long-term holders this never comes up. For people chasing ex-dates, it quietly kills the tax benefit.

My take: put the right fund in the right account

I've been building dividend income for over ten years and hit financial freedom at 31. Taxes are one of the few things I can actually control, so here's the simple framework I'd use:

  • Taxable account: qualified payers like $SCHD, $DGRO and $VOO. If your taxable income is under the 0% threshold, those dividends can be federal tax free.
  • IRA or Roth: the high yielders with mostly ordinary income, like $JEPI and $JEPQ. Let the big checks compound without the tax drag.
  • Return of capital funds ($QQQI, $SPYI): can work in taxable accounts for deferral, as long as the total return holds up. Track your cost basis.
  • $SGOV: fine in taxable, especially in a high tax state.

And always check your own 1099-DIV. These are 2025 numbers, and the 2026 split will be published early next year. Qualified dividend FAQ

Are VOO dividends qualified?

Almost all of them. Vanguard's 2025 tax data shows $VOO paid $7.0678 a share in ordinary dividends, and $6.8671 of that was qualified, about 97%. The small rest was Section 199A REIT dividends. $VOO paid no capital gains distributions in 2025.

Are SGOV dividends qualified or taxable?

$SGOV's dividends are taxable and 0% qualified, because they come from Treasury bill interest. They are taxed as ordinary income federally. The upside is that iShares reported 95.14% of $SGOV's 2025 income came from U.S. government obligations, which many states let you exclude from state income tax.

Is JEPQ a qualified dividend?

Mostly no. J.P. Morgan reported that only 5.10% of $JEPQ's 2025 income was qualified dividend income. For $JEPI the figure was 15.86%. The rest is generally taxed as ordinary income, which is why many investors hold these funds in an IRA or Roth.

How is QQQI taxed?

Based on NEOS's Form 8937 filings, about 99% of $QQQI's 2025 distributions were classified as return of capital, which is not taxed when received but lowers your cost basis. NEOS uses index options that are Section 1256 contracts, taxed 60% long term and 40% short term. $SPYI was about 95% return of capital in 2025.

How long do I have to hold an ETF for the dividends to be qualified?

Per IRS Publication 550, you must hold the shares for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. If you buy right before the ex-date and sell soon after, even a qualified payer's dividend gets taxed as ordinary income for you.
