Does VOO pay dividends? Schedule, yield and history (2026)

Yes. VOO pays a dividend every quarter, it just isn't a big one. Here's the 2026 payment schedule, how much it pays per share, why the yield is so low, and what that means if you're building income.

Short answer: Yes, $VOO pays dividends. It pays quarterly, usually at the very end of March, June, September and December. Over the last 12 months it paid $7.43 per share, which works out to a yield of about 1.1%. That's real income, but it's small. $VOO is a growth engine that happens to pay a dividend, not an income fund.

I get this question constantly, usually from people who just opened their first brokerage account and bought the S&P 500 because everyone told them to. Then a few dollars show up in their account at the end of the quarter and they're not sure what it is. That's the $VOO dividend.

The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF launched on September 7, 2010, holds every company in the S&P 500, and has grown to over $1 trillion in assets. Most of those companies pay dividends. Vanguard collects them and passes them through to you four times a year.

The pattern is very consistent. The ex-date lands in the last week or so of the quarter and the cash shows up two to four business days later. The last few December ex-dates were December 20, 2023, December 23, 2024 and December 22, 2025, so expect the next one right around the holidays.

One thing that trips people up: the payments are not the same every quarter. In 2026 the June payment was the biggest and September the smallest. That's normal. The amount depends on what the 500 companies inside actually paid during that quarter, so look at the full year, not one check.

So yes, it grows. From 2022 to 2025 the annual payout rose about 19%, or roughly 6% a year. That's not exciting on its own, but it's steady, and it happened without anybody at Vanguard choosing to raise anything. The companies in the index earned more and paid more.

Here's the part most people miss: the dividend per share went up, but the yield stayed tiny. That's because the share price went up even faster. That's a good problem to have.

Why VOO's dividend yield is so low

$VOO is weighted by market value, so the biggest companies get the biggest slice. Right now the top ten holdings make up about 39% of the fund, led by NVIDIA, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom and Meta. Those are some of the most valuable businesses on earth, and almost all of them pay little or nothing relative to their share price. They reinvest their cash into growth or buy back stock instead.

When nearly 40% of the fund barely pays a dividend, the whole fund's yield gets dragged down, even though hundreds of the smaller companies inside pay solid dividends. That's why a dedicated dividend fund like $SCHD, which I broke down in my full review, yields roughly three times as much. It deliberately owns cash-rich dividend payers and very little big tech.

I don't see the low yield as a flaw. It's just telling you what $VOO is for.

How VOO dividends are taxed, and should you reinvest?

Because $VOO owns ordinary U.S. companies, most of its dividends have historically been qualified dividends. In a taxable account, qualified dividends are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates for most people, not your regular income tax rate. In an IRA or 401(k), it doesn't matter until you withdraw. Your 1099-DIV each year is the final word on how your payments were classified.

As for reinvesting: if you don't need the income yet, I'd turn on dividend reinvestment and forget about it. With a 1% yield the dividend isn't going to change your life on its own, but automatically buying more shares four times a year adds up over decades, and it's one less decision you have to make.

If you're already retired or close to it and want the cash, that's a different conversation. That's usually where people start blending a core like $VOO with funds that pay more, often monthly. My take: who VOO's dividend is for

I've been a dividend investor for over a decade, and I still think $VOO is one of the best things a long-term investor can own. Just be clear-eyed about what it is.

  • If you're building wealth: $VOO's tiny dividend doesn't matter. Total return does, and that's where it's been excellent. Reinvest and let it run.
  • If you need income now: $VOO alone is a tough way to get there. At roughly 1% you need seven figures for a modest monthly check. You'd probably pair it with a dedicated dividend fund or an income ETF.
  • If you're somewhere in between: a mix is what a lot of people land on. A core of $VOO for growth, plus a dividend grower like $SCHD or $DGRO, plus a slice of higher income if you want cash flow today.

What I'd avoid is selling $VOO to chase a 15% yield because the dividend looked small. A big yield with a share price that keeps sliding can leave you worse off. VOO dividend FAQ

Does VOO pay dividends?

Yes. $VOO pays a dividend every quarter, usually at the end of March, June, September and December. It paid $7.43 per share over the 12 months through September 2026.

Does VOO pay dividends monthly?

No. $VOO pays quarterly, four times a year. If you want a monthly check, you would need to pair it with funds that pay in other months or use a monthly-paying ETF.

When is the next VOO dividend?

The next $VOO dividend is expected in late December 2026. In recent years the December ex-dividend date has landed between December 20 and December 23, with payment a few days later. Vanguard confirms the exact dates and amount shortly before.

What is VOO's dividend yield?

$VOO's dividend yield is currently about 1.1%, based on the TopDividendETFsPRO database, which updates daily.

Are VOO dividends qualified?

Most of $VOO's dividends have historically been qualified, because they come from regular U.S. companies. Qualified dividends held in a taxable account are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates. Your 1099-DIV shows the exact split for your payments.
