The Vanguard International High Dividend Yield ETF launched on February 25, 2016. Think of it as the overseas cousin of $VYM: same basic idea (own the higher-yielding half of the market, weighted by size), but with every company outside the U.S.

And here's the thing most U.S. dividend investors miss. Because foreign stocks trade at lower valuations and pay out more of their profits, $VYMI yields noticeably more than $VYM, and even more than $SCHD right now. So let's look at whether that extra income is worth it.

Why the VYMI dividend jumps around so much

Look at those 2026 numbers again. $0.71, then $1.26, then $0.82. That's not a mistake, and it's not a cut. It's how international dividends work.

Most U.S. companies pay quarterly, so a fund like $VYM gets a pretty even stream. A lot of European, Japanese and Australian companies pay once or twice a year instead, and many of those big payments come in the spring. $VYMI collects all of that and passes it on, so the June check is reliably the biggest of the year and March is usually the smallest. It's been that way every single year in the data below.

Currency matters too. $VYMI's companies pay in euros, pounds, yen, francs and so on, and Vanguard converts that into dollars. When the dollar is weak, your dividend gets a boost. When the dollar is strong, it shrinks, even if the companies didn't change a thing. That's a real source of income volatility you don't get with a U.S.-only fund.

My advice: judge $VYMI on the full year, never on one quarter. If you budget around this income, plan for the yearly total and let June cover the lighter months.

What VYMI holds

$VYMI tracks the FTSE All-World ex US High Dividend Yield Index. It takes stocks from developed and emerging markets outside the U.S., keeps the ones expected to pay above-average dividends over the next year, and weights them by market cap. As of September 30, 2026, that was 1,553 holdings.

VYMI taxes: foreign withholding and qualified dividends

This is the part people forget to think about. Most countries take a cut of dividends paid to foreign investors before the money ever reaches the fund. That's called foreign withholding tax, and it's already baked into the dividends you see.

In a regular taxable brokerage account, you can usually get some of that back. Vanguard reports the foreign tax paid on your 1099-DIV, and you can claim the foreign tax credit on your return. In an IRA or 401(k), you generally can't claim it, so that withholding is just gone. That's one reason a lot of investors hold international dividend funds in taxable accounts and keep U.S. funds in retirement accounts. Ask a tax pro what makes sense for you.

As for qualified dividends, dividends from many foreign companies can qualify for the lower long-term capital gains rates (for example, companies in countries with a U.S. tax treaty), as long as you meet the holding period. But the qualified share is typically lower than a U.S. fund like $DGRO or $VYM. Vanguard publishes the exact qualified percentage each tax season, and your 1099-DIV is the final word.

$VYMI vs $VYM: same family, same low-cost Vanguard approach. $VYMI pays meaningfully more and its payout has grown faster lately. $VYM has the longer track record and the more even quarterly checks. They actually work great together: one covers the U.S., one covers everything else.

$VYMI vs $SCHD: $SCHD has crushed it on total return since launch, and I break down why in my full SCHD review. But $SCHD is 100 U.S. stocks. If all of your dividend income comes from one country, $VYMI is the simplest way to diversify that.

$VYMI vs $IDV: $IDV yields more, but look at the price decay flag and the much lower long-run return. It's more concentrated in the highest yielders and costs 0.50%. This is a classic case where I'd rather own the lower yield with a healthy share price than chase the bigger number.

My take: is VYMI a good investment for income?

I've been building dividend income for over a decade, and the one mistake I see over and over is people owning only U.S. stocks without ever deciding to. $VYMI is the easiest fix I know for that, and it pays you better than the U.S. version while you wait.

  • You want a higher yield without junk: about 3.7%, 1,500+ companies, a 0.07% fee and no price decay.
  • Your dividend income is all U.S.: $VYMI spreads it across Europe, Japan, Canada, Australia and emerging markets.
  • You invest in a taxable account: that's where you can usually claim the foreign tax credit.

Where I'd be careful: don't buy $VYMI if uneven quarterly checks will bother you, because they will be uneven. And know that the dollar can work against you for years at a time. International stocks also went through a long stretch of lagging the U.S., and that can happen again.

For me, the right way to think about it is as a piece, not the whole thing. A core U.S. dividend fund plus a slice of $VYMI is a simple, cheap, diversified income setup. VYMI dividend FAQ

How often does VYMI pay dividends?

$VYMI pays quarterly, with ex-dividend dates in the second half of March, June, September and December. The cash usually arrives two to four business days after the ex-date.

How much does VYMI pay per share?

$VYMI paid $3.72 per share over the last 12 months. In 2026 the quarterly payments have been $0.708 in March, $1.2569 in June and $0.817 in September.

Why is the VYMI June dividend so much bigger?

Many international companies pay dividends once or twice a year, often in the spring, instead of every quarter. $VYMI passes those payments through, so the June payout has been the largest of the year every year since at least 2022. Currency moves also change the dollar amount.

How much does $10,000 in VYMI pay?

At a yield of about 3.7%, $10,000 in $VYMI pays roughly $370 a year, split into four uneven quarterly payments. You can model your own amount with the free VYMI dividend calculator on DividendProjection.

Does VYMI have foreign withholding tax?

Yes. Many countries withhold tax on dividends paid to foreign investors, and that is already taken out of $VYMI's payouts. In a taxable account you can usually claim the foreign tax credit using the amount on your 1099-DIV; in an IRA you generally cannot.
