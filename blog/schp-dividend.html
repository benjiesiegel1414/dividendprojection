The Schwab U.S. TIPS ETF launched on August 5, 2010. It owns one thing: U.S. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, better known as TIPS. These are government bonds whose principal goes up with inflation, so the income is tied to the cost of living instead of being fixed.

That makes $SCHP a very different animal from the dividend funds I usually write about. It's not a stock fund and it's not trying to grow your money fast. It's insurance against inflation, and the way it pays you reflects that. If you've looked at its dividend history and thought something was broken, it isn't. Let me show you why.

Why SCHP skips months and swings so much

This is the part most people miss. A TIPS bond pays you two ways:

  • A small fixed coupon. The weighted average coupon in $SCHP was only about 1.36% as of September 30, 2026, according to Schwab.
  • An inflation adjustment. The bond's principal is adjusted with the Consumer Price Index. When inflation runs hot, the principal grows and that growth flows into the fund's income. When inflation is low or the index dips, the adjustment shrinks or even goes negative.

The fund passes all of that through to you. So in months when the inflation adjustment is strong, the check is big. In months when it's weak or negative, it can wipe out the coupon income, and $SCHP simply doesn't pay. That's what happened in the first three months of 2026, and in January and February of 2025.

The cleanest way to think about it: your $SCHP income is a rough mirror of recent inflation. Summer 2026 paid over 20 cents a month. Spring and fall paid a fraction of that. That's the product doing exactly what it's designed to do.

So if you need a smooth, predictable monthly check to pay bills, $SCHP is the wrong tool. For that, a T-bill fund like $SGOV pays far more evenly. Judge $SCHP on the full year, never on one month.

But here's the honest caveat: "growth" isn't really the right word for a TIPS fund. The payout goes up when inflation goes up and down when it cools. It's not like $SCHD, where companies raise dividends year after year. Treat a big year as a sign inflation was high, not as a raise.

The fee is the best part: 0.03%, about $3 a year on $10,000. That's as cheap as it gets for this kind of fund.

Is the SCHP dividend qualified? How it's taxed

No. $SCHP's distributions come from bond interest and inflation adjustments, not company dividends, so they're generally taxed as ordinary income, not at the lower qualified dividend rates.

Two things work in your favor, though:

  • State taxes. Interest from U.S. Treasury securities is exempt from state and local income tax. Many states let you exclude the part of a fund's distributions that came from Treasuries, but the rules vary by state, so check your 1099-DIV and your state's rules.
  • The ETF avoids "phantom income." If you own individual TIPS in a taxable account, you can owe tax on the inflation adjustment even though you don't get that cash until the bond matures. The ETF pays the income out, so you actually receive cash for what you're taxed on.

Because it's ordinary income, a lot of investors keep TIPS funds in an IRA or 401(k), where that doesn't matter. That's how I'd think about placement too. Ask a tax pro what fits your situation.

$SCHP vs $TIP: nearly the same job, owning the broad TIPS market. The difference is cost. $TIP charges 0.18%, six times $SCHP's 0.03%. On $10,000 that's $18 a year versus $3, forever. On a fund whose whole edge is a few percent of income, I'd take the cheaper one.

My take: is SCHP a good investment for income?

I've been building dividend income for over a decade, and I reached financial freedom mostly through stock dividend ETFs, not bonds. So I look at $SCHP as a specific tool, not an income engine.

  • You want inflation protection on safe money: that's exactly what TIPS do, and $SCHP does it for 0.03%.
  • You're near or in retirement: inflation is one of the biggest long-term risks to a fixed budget, and a slice of TIPS helps cover it.
  • You hold it in an IRA or 401(k): the ordinary income tax treatment matters a lot less there.

Where I'd be careful: don't buy $SCHP for its current yield. A big part of that yield is recent inflation passing through, and it will come down when inflation does. Don't use it if you need a reliable monthly check, because some months it pays nothing. And don't expect growth. $10,000 at launch is about $15,000 today, while a dividend grower like $SCHD did many times that.

For me, the right way to use it is as the inflation-hedge piece of the safe side of a portfolio, next to cash in something like $SGOV, with the long-term income coming from dividend ETFs like $VYM or $SCHD. SCHP dividend FAQ

Does SCHP pay dividends monthly?

Yes, $SCHP is a monthly payer, with ex-dates at the start of each month. But it does not pay every month. When the inflation adjustment on its TIPS is weak or negative, there can be no distribution, which happened in January, February and March of 2026.

How much does SCHP pay per month?

It varies a lot. In 2026 $SCHP's monthly payments ranged from $0 to $0.2214 per share, and it paid $1.25 per share over the last 12 months. At a yield of about 5.1%, $10,000 pays roughly $42.50 a month on average.

Why did SCHP not pay a dividend in January?

$SCHP's income comes from TIPS coupons plus inflation adjustments to the bonds' principal. When inflation readings are low or negative, the adjustment can offset the coupon income, leaving nothing to distribute. $SCHP made no payment in January or February of 2025 or 2026.

Are SCHP dividends qualified?

No. $SCHP's distributions come from Treasury bond interest and inflation adjustments, so they are generally taxed as ordinary income. Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax, and many states let you exclude that portion of a fund's distributions.

What is SCHP dividend history?

$SCHP paid $1.07 per share across 11 payments in 2025 and $0.98 across 8 payments from January through November 2026, about 21% more than the same months of 2025. The last 12 months totaled $1.25 per share.
