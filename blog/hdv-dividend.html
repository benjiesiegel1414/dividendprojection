The iShares Core High Dividend ETF launched on March 29, 2011. It owns about 75 high-yielding U.S. stocks that pass Morningstar's quality and financial health screens, and it's one of the cheapest high-dividend funds out there at 0.08% a year.

2026 was a big year for $HDV holders, and not because of anything the companies did. The fund split its shares 5-for-1 in April and moved to monthly payouts in the summer. If you looked at your brokerage account and thought your dividend got slashed, it didn't. Let me walk through it.

Why HDV's dividend looks smaller: the split and the switch to monthly

Two changes landed in 2026, and together they make $HDV's dividend history look like a disaster if you don't know what happened.

  • A 5-for-1 split on April 29, 2026. iShares filed it with the SEC in March. Every share became five shares, each worth about a fifth as much. The March 2026 dividend was $0.842962 per old share, which is $0.168592 per new share. Same money, smaller slices.
  • Quarterly to monthly in June 2026. iShares' distribution schedule lists $HDV as changing frequency from quarterly to monthly on June 16, 2026, with the first monthly payment going ex-dividend July 15. Now the same yearly income is cut into 12 checks instead of 4.

Neither change adds or removes a dollar of income on its own. If you owned 100 shares before, you now own 500, and you get paid 12 times a year instead of 4. That's it.

All the per-share amounts in this guide are split-adjusted, which is how iShares shows them on the fund page now. If you compare them to an older article, divide the old numbers by five.

So far in 2026, $HDV has paid $0.708 a share across six payments. Every one of them was 100% income, with zero return of capital. That's what you want to see from a stock dividend fund.

Here's the thing about the monthly checks: they swing a lot. August paid $0.105, September dropped to $0.056, and October bounced back to $0.106. That's not a cut. Most of the companies $HDV owns pay quarterly, on their own schedules, so some months the fund collects a lot of cash and some months it collects less. It just passes through what comes in.

My advice: budget off the yearly total, not any single month.

Great for your account balance, but it means new buyers today lock in a lower yield than someone who bought five years ago.

How HDV picks its stocks

$HDV tracks the Morningstar Dividend Yield Focus Index. According to Morningstar's index rulebook, it works like this:

  • Only qualified dividend payers. REITs are excluded, because their dividends usually aren't qualified. This is why $HDV's dividends are so tax-friendly.
  • A quality screen. Companies have to pass Morningstar's economic moat and uncertainty ratings and rank well on its Distance to Default measure, a model of how likely a company is to get into financial trouble. This is meant to weed out the yield traps.
  • The top 75 by yield. Of the survivors, the 75 highest yielders make the cut. iShares listed 73 holdings on November 13, 2026.
  • Weighted by dividend dollars. Companies that pay out the most total cash get the biggest weights, with caps so no single stock goes over 10% and the stocks above 5% can't add up to more than half the fund.

That last rule is the big one. Weighting by total dividend dollars means the giant payers, think Exxon and Chevron, end up dominating. That's very different from $SCHD, which caps every stock at 4%.

By sector, it's consumer staples (26.9%) and energy (23.7%), then health care (13.8%), utilities (11.2%) and consumer discretionary (10.2%). Tech is just 0.4%. Financials are only 4.7%, which is the opposite of $DVY's bank-heavy mix.

So when you buy $HDV, about half your money is in oil, tobacco, soda and household staples. iShares lists a 3-year equity beta of just 0.20, which means it has barely moved with the broad market. If your core is an S&P 500 fund like $VOO, $HDV is a real diversifier. It's also why it can lag hard in a tech rally, and why oil prices matter a lot to it.

Are HDV dividends qualified?

Yes. According to iShares' 2025 QDI summary, 100% of $HDV's 2025 dividends were qualified dividend income. That makes sense, since the index only picks stocks whose dividends count as qualified income and skips REITs.

In a taxable account, qualified dividends get the lower long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15% or 20% federally, depending on your income) instead of your ordinary income rate, as long as you meet the holding period. The monthly switch doesn't change that. I covered the same question for $DGRO, which was also 100% qualified. Your exact numbers will be in Box 1b of your 1099-DIV. This isn't tax advice, so check with a tax pro.

The tradeoffs: what you give up with HDV

  • Concentration. Over half the fund is in 10 stocks, and Exxon and Chevron alone are almost 16%. One bad year for oil shows up fast.
  • Sector bets. Staples and energy are about 50% of the fund. Almost no tech, almost no banks. That's a feature if you want balance, a bug if you want growth.
  • Slow dividend growth. Roughly 2.8% a year from 2021 to 2025, with a dip in 2025. That's about inflation, not much more.
  • Lumpy monthly checks. September 2026 paid about half of what August did. If you need the same amount every month, keep a small cash buffer.

None of these are deal breakers. They're just what you're signing up for when you buy a concentrated, high-yield, low-cost fund.

My take: who HDV is for

I've been building dividend income for more than ten years, and I like funds where the income is real, the fee is tiny and the rules are simple. $HDV checks all three. 100% qualified dividends, no return of capital, 0.08% a year.

  • If you want monthly income from real dividends: $HDV is now one of the cleanest options. No options strategy, no leverage, just big companies paying cash.
  • If you want dividend growth: I'd rather own $SCHD or $DGRO. $HDV's payout has barely kept up with inflation.
  • If you're heavy in tech: $HDV's energy and staples tilt is about as different from the S&P 500 as a dividend fund gets.
  • If you need high income right now: 3.1% won't get you there alone. That's a different kind of fund, with different risks.

HDV dividend FAQ

Does HDV pay dividends monthly?

Yes, since mid-2026. iShares changed $HDV from quarterly to monthly distributions on June 16, 2026, and the first monthly payment went ex-dividend July 15, 2026. It now goes ex-dividend on the third Wednesday of each month, except December.

When is the next HDV dividend?

According to iShares' distribution schedule, the next $HDV dividend goes ex-dividend November 18, 2026 and is paid November 23. The December dividend goes ex on December 15 and is paid December 18, 2026.

Why did HDV's dividend drop in 2026?

It didn't, really. $HDV split 5-for-1 on April 29, 2026 and then switched from quarterly to monthly payments. Each share is now a fifth of the old size and each check covers about a month instead of a quarter, so the per-share amounts look much smaller. Are HDV dividends qualified?

Yes. iShares' 2025 QDI summary shows 100% of $HDV's 2025 dividends were qualified dividend income. Its index only includes stocks whose dividends are qualified income and excludes REITs.

What is HDV's dividend yield?
