The iShares Select Dividend ETF launched on November 3, 2003, which makes it one of the oldest dividend ETFs still around. It owns about 100 of the higher-yielding U.S. stocks and weights them by the dividends they pay, not by how big the company is.

That one design choice explains almost everything about $DVY: why it yields more than $VYM or $SDY, why it leans so hard into banks and utilities, and why it barely owns any tech.

So far in 2026, $DVY has paid $3.73 a share across three payments. That's about 5.6% more than the same three payments in 2025 ($3.53). Each 2026 check has also been bigger than the one before it, which is a nice trend to see.

When is the next DVY dividend?

Over the last two years, $DVY has gone ex-dividend in the middle of the quarter's last month: March 18, June 16, September 16 and December 16 in 2025, then March 17, June 15 and September 15 in 2026.

Following that pattern, I'd expect the next ex-date in mid-December 2026, around December 15, with the cash a few days later. iShares hasn't announced the amount yet, so treat the date as an estimate until it does.

Here's the part worth planning around: the December check has been the big one. It was $1.616 in December 2025, versus $1.049 the March before. In 2024, September was the largest ($1.55). Point being, $DVY's quarterly checks are lumpy. If you budget off a single quarter, you'll either overestimate or underestimate your income.

From 2021 to 2025 the payout grew about 35%, or roughly 7.7% a year. That's honestly better than a lot of funds with "dividend growth" in the name. For comparison, $SDY's payout grew about 4.4% a year from 2022 to 2025.

Why? Because $DVY weights by dividends paid. When a company raises its dividend, its weight in the index tends to go up at the next rebalance. The fund naturally leans into the companies sending out more cash.

One honest note: the flip side is that high-yield funds get hit hardest when dividends get cut across the market. High yielders tend to be mature, slower-growing businesses, and when a recession forces cuts, they're often first in line. Four good years is a trend, not a promise.

How DVY picks its stocks

$DVY tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Select Dividend Index. iShares describes it simply as an index of relatively high dividend yielding U.S. stocks. Here's how I'd explain it:

  • Start with dividend payers that have a track record. Companies need a multi-year history of paying dividends and have to pass screens on dividend growth and payout ratio, which help filter out companies paying more than they can afford.
  • Pick roughly 100 of the highest yielders. As of November 12, 2026, $DVY held 98 stocks.
  • Weight by dividend, not by size. This is the big one. A company's slice of the fund depends on how much it pays out, so a mid-sized bank with a fat dividend can outweigh a much bigger company with a small one.

This is the part most people miss: there's no long dividend growth streak required, unlike $SDY's 20-year rule or $NOBL's 25-year rule. $DVY is a high-dividend fund with some quality guardrails, not a dividend aristocrat fund. You get more income today, and you accept a portfolio that looks very different from the S&P 500.

By sector, it's financials (25.3%) and utilities (22.6%), then consumer staples (13.8%) and energy (10.1%). Tech is only 3.0% and industrials 1.9%. Nearly half the fund sits in banks, insurers, asset managers and utilities.

If your core holding is an S&P 500 fund like $VOO, which is dominated by tech, $DVY is close to its opposite. iShares lists a 3-year equity beta of just 0.49, meaning it has moved about half as much as the broad market. That's a real diversifier, and also why it lagged during big tech-led rallies.

The tradeoffs: what you give up with DVY

  • The fee. At 0.38%, you pay $38 a year per $10,000. $SCHD costs $6 and $VYM $4. Over 20 or 30 years, that gap compounds into real money.
  • Sector concentration. Close to half the fund is financials and utilities. A rough stretch for banks or a spike in interest rates (which tends to pressure utilities) hits $DVY harder than a broad fund.
  • Very little growth exposure. With tech around 3%, you'll miss most of any big tech rally. That's been the main reason $DVY has trailed the S&P 500 over long stretches.
  • Lumpy checks. Quarterly amounts can swing by 30% or more. Fine if you look at the year, annoying if you budget by the quarter.

None of these are deal breakers. They're just the price of the higher, faster-growing income. You should know what you're paying for.

My take: who DVY is for

I've been building dividend income for more than ten years, and I like funds where I understand exactly why the income is what it is. $DVY passes that test. It owns high yielders, weights them by what they pay, and the payout has grown at a healthy clip.

  • If you want higher income from real dividends: $DVY is a reasonable pick. No options, no return of capital games, just companies paying cash.
  • If you care most about cost: $SCHD and $HDV deliver a similar yield for a fraction of the fee. That's hard to ignore over decades.
  • If you're already heavy in tech: $DVY's tilt toward financials, utilities and staples is one of the cleanest diversifiers out there.
  • If you need high income right now: 3.3% won't get you there by itself. That's a different kind of fund, with different risks.

DVY dividend FAQ

How often does DVY pay dividends?

$DVY pays quarterly, four times a year. In 2025 and 2026 it went ex-dividend in the middle of March, June, September and December, with the cash arriving a few days later.

When is the next DVY dividend?

The most recent $DVY dividend was $1.329 per share, ex-dividend September 15, 2026. Based on its pattern, the next one should go ex-dividend in mid-December 2026, around December 15. Last year's December payment of $1.616 was the biggest of the year.

What is DVY's dividend yield?

$DVY's dividend yield is currently about 3.3%, based on the TopDividendETFsPRO database, which updates daily. It paid $5.34 per share over the last 12 months. iShares listed a 12-month trailing yield of 3.29% and a 30-day SEC yield of 3.40% as of October 31, 2026.

Is DVY better than SCHD?

They pay a similar yield, but $SCHD charges 0.06% a year versus 0.38% for $DVY and has a higher annualized total return since its 2011 launch. $DVY's edge is a heavier tilt toward utilities and financials and a long track record going back to 2003. Many investors see them as different flavors of high-dividend investing rather than direct substitutes.

How much does $10,000 in DVY pay?

At the current yield of about 3.3%, $10,000 in $DVY pays about $330 a year, or roughly $82.50 per quarter on average. The checks are uneven: the September and December payments have usually been larger than March and June.
