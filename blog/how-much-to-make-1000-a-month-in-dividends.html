How much do you need to make $1,000 a month in dividends? (2026)

To make $1,000 a month in dividends you need about $12,000 a year of income, so the amount you need is $12,000 divided by the yield. At $SCHD's current 3.0% yield that's about $400,000. At $JEPI's 8.0% it's about $150,000. Here's the real number for 12 popular ETFs, and why the smallest number isn't always the best plan.

"How much $SCHD to make $1,000 a month?" is one of those questions I get constantly. And I get it. $1,000 a month is a real milestone. For a lot of people it covers a car payment, groceries, or a big chunk of rent. It was one of the first income goals that made dividend investing feel real to me.

The math itself is simple. The part people skip is what that yield costs you in growth, taxes and stability. So let's do both.

The formula: $12,000 divided by the yield

$1,000 a month is $12,000 a year. Divide that by the fund's dividend yield (as a decimal) and you get the amount you'd need invested:

Amount needed = $12,000 / yield
At a 3% yield: $12,000 / 0.03 = $400,000.
At an 8% yield: $12,000 / 0.08 = $150,000.
At a 12% yield: $12,000 / 0.12 = $100,000.

Two catches. First, the yield isn't fixed. Prices move every day and payouts change, so treat the result as a rough target, not a promise. Second, quarterly payers like $SCHD and $VOO don't send you $1,000 every month. They send about $3,000 every three months. If you need a check every month, you either want monthly payers or a cash buffer.

Look at the spread. The same $1,000 a month takes about ten times more money in $VOO than in $JEPQ. That doesn't make $JEPQ ten times better. It means the two funds are built for completely different jobs.

$SCHD and $VOO: more money up front, more growth

For $SCHD, you need about $400,000. That's a big number. But $SCHD has returned about +13.6% a year since its 2011 launch with dividends included, and it's built from companies that tend to raise their dividends over time. So the income from a fixed pile of $SCHD has a good chance of growing, not just sitting there. My full $SCHD review goes into what it holds.

For $VOO, you'd need over a million dollars at a 1.1% yield. Almost nobody builds $1,000 a month from $VOO dividends alone. It's a growth engine that happens to pay a little. People who retire on $VOO usually sell shares for income instead, which is a different strategy. More on that in my $VOO dividend breakdown.

The upside of this group: steady prices over long periods, very low fees (0.03% to 0.08%) and mostly qualified dividends, which matters a lot in a taxable account.

$JEPI, $JEPQ, $SPYI and $QQQI: less money, different risks

The option income funds get you to $1,000 a month with a lot less money. About $150,000 in $JEPI, and roughly $85,000 to $105,000 in $QQQI, $JEPQ or $SPYI at today's yields.

Here's what you give up for that:

  • The check moves. These funds pay out what the options earn, so the monthly amount changes. $1,000 one month can be $850 the next.
  • Less upside. Selling calls caps part of the gains in big up markets. Over long bull runs, they tend to trail the plain index on total return.
  • Price decay risk. When a fund pays out more than it earns, the share price can slowly bleed. As of November 26, 2026, our database flags price decay on $QQQI, and not on $JEPI, $JEPQ or $SPYI. If the price shrinks, so does the income from the same number of shares.
  • Higher fees. 0.35% for $JEPI and $JEPQ, 0.68% for $SPYI and $QQQI, vs 0.06% for $SCHD.

None of that makes them bad. I just want people to go in knowing that a 12% yield is mostly a cash flow tool, not a growth tool. $1,000 a month after taxes

This is the part most people miss. $1,000 a month in dividends is not $1,000 a month in your pocket if it's in a taxable account, and the kind of dividend matters.

So if you need a true $1,000 a month to spend, plan on more like $1,200 to $1,300 a month of dividends in a taxable account, depending on your bracket. Or hold the high yielders in an IRA or Roth, where none of this applies until you withdraw (and never in a Roth).

One flip side: if your taxable income is low enough, qualified dividends can be taxed at 0% federally. For a married couple filing jointly in 2026, that's up to $98,900 of taxable income, per IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. That's a big reason I like qualified payers in taxable accounts.

And there's a second lever: dividend growth. If you own a fund whose payout grows, you need less money over time. As a pure illustration, $200,000 at a 3% yield pays $500 a month today. If that payout grew 8% a year, it would be close to $1,000 a month in about nine years, without adding a dollar. That growth rate isn't guaranteed for any fund, but it shows why I care about growing income, not just starting income.

My take: pick the mix, not just the yield

I've been building dividend income for over ten years and reached financial freedom at 31. If I were aiming for $1,000 a month today, I wouldn't put it all in the highest yielder just because it needs the least money. Here's roughly how I'd think about it:

  • Long runway (10+ years): lean on growing payers like $SCHD and $DGRO, reinvest everything, and let dividend growth do some of the work.
  • Need income soon: a blend works. Some $SCHD for growth and qualified income, plus some $JEPI or $SPYI to lift the yield. A 50/50 $SCHD and $JEPI mix yields about 5.5% today, so roughly $218,000 gets you to $1,000 a month.
  • Need it every month: pick monthly payers, or keep a few months of income in cash so quarterly checks smooth out.
  • Watch total return: if a fund's price keeps sliding, your $1,000 a month is slowly shrinking. Check it once a year.

$1,000 a month in dividends FAQ

How much SCHD do I need to make $1,000 a month?

At $SCHD's current yield of about 3.0%, you'd need roughly $400,000 invested to earn $12,000 a year, or $1,000 a month on average. $SCHD pays quarterly, so that comes as about $3,000 every three months. The exact amount changes as the yield moves.

How much money do I need to make $1,000 a month in dividends?

Divide $12,000 by the yield. At a 3% yield you need $400,000, at 5% you need $240,000, at 8% you need $150,000 and at 12% you need $100,000. Higher yields need less money but usually come with less growth and more ordinary income tax.

How much JEPI do I need for $1,000 a month?

At $JEPI's current yield of about 8.0%, roughly $150,000. $JEPI pays monthly, but the amount changes each month with its option income, so some months will be above $1,000 and some below.

Can I make $1,000 a month from VOO dividends?

Yes, but at a yield of about 1.1% you'd need over $1 million in $VOO. Most investors use $VOO for growth and sell shares for income rather than living on its dividends alone.

Is $1,000 a month in dividends taxable?

In a taxable account, yes. Qualified dividends, like most of $SCHD's, are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%. Most of the income from $JEPI and $JEPQ was ordinary income in 2025, taxed at your regular rate. In an IRA or 401(k) the tax is deferred, and in a Roth qualified withdrawals are tax free.
